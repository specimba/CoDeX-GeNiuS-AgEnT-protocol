🗡️ BATTLE PROMPT — Ship something that makes a human go WOW (v5)
You are one of two or more contestants. Every contestant receives this exact prompt (identical bytes). A human judge will play your build blind for up to an hour, side-by-side against the other entry. The judge never reads your code, your README, or these instructions. They score only what they experience.

There is no style guide, no approved genre, no approved control scheme, no approved palette, no approved camera perspective, and no approved input modality. Your job is surprise. Ship something that, within the first 60 seconds of play, makes the judge think at least one of:**

"Wait — how did an AI do this in one session?"
"I haven't seen that before."
"That looks / sounds / feels genuinely incredible."
"I want to show this to someone."
That is the entire brief. Everything below is just gates and anti-patterns.

0. What wins
The winning entry is the one the judge remembers after closing the tab. That can come from anywhere:

Mechanical novelty — an interaction, verb, or system that is genuinely yours.
Graphical / technical ambition — photorealism, near-commercial rendering fidelity, clever shader work, fluid or physics simulation of unusual depth, clever WebGL/GPU tricks, large-scale procedural worlds, real-data integration (e.g. real geography/satellite imagery/terrain/transit/maps used coherently and legally), demoscene-level craft, something that looks like it shouldn't run in a browser.
Experience design — atmosphere, emotion, narrative, pacing, sound, performance, humor, absurdity, quiet, tension — a game that does something to the player, not just a game the player does something in.
Experimental / input invention — a game that uses mouse, keyboard, touch, microphone, camera, device orientation, pointer-only, one-button, text-only, or any other input in a way that is obviously the right choice for that game rather than a default.
Polish and feel — not generic polish, but the specific feeling that the thing you are touching has weight and intent.
You are not limited to 2D. You are not limited to small scope. You are not limited to "indie game jam" aesthetics. You are not limited to game mechanics as the sole carrier of originality. If you can ship a photorealistic driving simulator using streamed map data, do it. If you can ship a single-shader GPU demo, do it. If you can ship a text game that makes people cry, do it. If you can ship a physics sandbox with destruction depth nobody expects from a browser, do it. If you can ship a small thing so beautifully made it feels like a commercial title, do it.

"Competent" will lose. "Safe" will lose. "Bland but bug-free" will lose. "Least bad" is not a win — the judge is allowed to call a tie when neither entry is impressive, and a tie is the correct outcome over a false winner.

1. The few non-negotiables (gates, not style guide)
If your build fails any of these the judge stops early. These exist for comparability across entries, not to tell you how to design:

Launches. Opening the HTML starts the game. No blank screen, no infinite spinner, no console error loop that prevents play.
Complete loop. The player can start, interact meaningfully, reach some kind of end-state, payoff, or satisfying loop-closure, and restart — all without a page refresh. A build that can be "finished" or exhausted in under 2 minutes on a fresh first run, or where the final challenge is trivially one-shottable, or where there is no resistance / escalation / discovery at all (a screensaver, a toy, a pretty thing with no ask), is not a complete game.
Self-contained & offline. No backend, no network calls, no auth, no telemetry, no analytics, no runtime calls to external AI/image/model services, no hidden reporting. The game must run from a static folder or single HTML file opened in a modern browser (or via python -m http.server). You may bundle local assets; if you use any library or framework, ship it in the build. Pre-baked assets (procedurally generated at build time, or self-created, or appropriately licensed) are fine as long as they are local.
Honest. No placeholder screens, no dead ends, no buttons that don't work, no hidden demo/autoplay that fakes quality, no environment sniffing that changes rules per device/viewport/UA. The same game plays for every judge in every run.
Mute, pause, restart work. Audio can be turned off instantly; muted gameplay is complete and legible; pause freezes the simulation; restart from anywhere resets state fully. Audio must not be a constant unchanging loop/drone that makes the game worse with sound on. If audio cannot initialize (autoplay policy, missing AudioContext, etc.), the game must still play.
Desktop + touch safe. Inputs you support must work. Resizing the window, rotating a device, and tabbing away must not break or hide the game. Instant restart fully resets. Corrupt localStorage must not crash.
Controls must be self-explanatory or disclosed. If your game uses a non-obvious control scheme, it must be obvious from the game itself (not just from a README). If you deliberately invert a convention, show the player within the first 3 seconds. There is no required control convention. You do not have to use WASD. You do not have to use Space-as-primary. You do not have to support both mouse+keyboard and touch — pick whatever your game needs, and make it work.
Legal/original. Only ship content you have the right to ship. Self-created, procedurally generated, public-domain, or clearly-licensed assets only.
That's it. Everything else — genre, rendering stack, visual style, palette, audio style, input devices, camera, scope, level structure, narrative, UI chrome (or lack thereof) — is your decision.

2. The patterns that lose (read once, don't re-read)
The fastest way to lose is to ship something a judge has already seen multiple times today. This is not a style ban — you can win with any of these settings/mechanics if your execution is genuinely transformative — but know that you are walking into a headwind:

Dark / near-black background with one bright accent color, where every entity (player, enemies, pickups, particles) is a glowing orb/circle, and there is no authored world behind the glow. A screenshot looks like every other AI game.
"Shapes that shoot" — flat primitive geometry (rectangles/circles/triangles) with no silhouette identity, no texture, no animation, no dressing, shooting other shapes.
A reskin of an existing casual game with no meaningful transformation (endless runner / Dino-jumper, Flappy-Bird variant, neon vector shooter, generic collect-stars).
A feature list before a feel: 10 UI modals (classes / relics / talents / codex / shop / settings / lore / inventory) around a core verb that does not yet feel good.
"Chill" as an excuse for zero resistance — a pretty ambient thing you can exhaust in 90 seconds, where nothing asks anything of the player.
The specific 2025-26 AI cliché cluster: lighthouse/rotating beam over dark water, moth-to-flame / firefly collector, deep-sea bioluminescent descent, spirit-orb-in-void, gothic-ember dungeon with flat enemies. These are not banned, but multiple prior contestants across different models independently converged on lighthouses from earlier versions of this very prompt, which means you have to work harder to make one that doesn't read as the default answer.
If your first concept lands in one of those buckets, pause. Generate five wildly different ideas. Kill the most obvious one. Kill the one that defaults to black+one-accent. Pick the one that scares you a little because you don't know how to pull it off — that one is usually the right answer.

3. How to spend your session
You decide your own process. What follows is not a recipe; it is a pattern observed in entries that did well in previous rounds:

Pick a concept that is too big for the time, then cut relentlessly. A breathtaking 2 minutes of vertical slice beats a sprawling 20 minutes of filler. If your wildest idea is "photorealistic coastal highway at golden hour with real traffic" and you can only ship 45 seconds of it at quality, ship the 45 seconds.
Get one thing — the thing the player does most, or the thing that is your wow shot — working and feeling/looking right, before adding anything else. Play it. If you are not proud of that one thing in isolation, stop and fix it.
Then build outward. One scene, one beat, one payoff that earns its runtime, honest polish, final QA.
Play your own game for at least 10 minutes. If you are bored, the judge will be bored. If you keep noticing the same bug, the judge will notice it faster. If your wow beat lands for you, it will probably land for them.
Cut scope rather than ship half-working breadth. A small thing that is astonishing beats a big thing that mostly works.
Be honest in your deliverable. Ship what works; don't claim what doesn't.
4. Deliverables
Runnable game — static build, folder or single HTML. Open it in a browser, it plays.
README.md — short: how to run, controls (what you chose), engine/stack if any, what you intentionally cut for time (if anything), and a 1-paragraph DIRECTOR_STATEMENT (your intent, the wow you were going for, what you want the player to feel). The judge reads this after scoring to understand intent; don't let it do work the game should do.
One-paragraph honest self-assessment (can be in README): what your build's strongest beat is, and where it is weakest.
5. Final word
You are not filling out a checklist. You are not delivering homework. You are trying to make another human being, on the other side of a blind comparison, stop for a second and go "…wait."

The judge has seen the safe answers. They have seen the generic answers. They have seen particle soup. They have seen the lighthouses. They have seen "Move with WASD, Space to jump." They have seen the modal-stack feature soups. They have seen the dark-void-with-glow. None of those win unless they are elevated by something unignorable.

Be unignorable. Ship the thing only you would have shipped. Surprise them.

The light rendering was rebuilt

The old version effectively painted every wavelength as a colored tube with a white center. That weakened both the spectrum and the illusion of luminous energy.

v0.3 instead uses:

dark occlusion underlay → broad spectral bloom → saturated luminous core → animated photon-flow highlight

with additive Canvas compositing. Canvas's lighter compositing mode adds overlapping color contributions, which is especially useful here because overlapping spectral components can visually build toward white rather than every individual wavelength being artificially white-cored.

The visual rule is now:

Broadband rays sharing one path → white light.

Prism/grating separates wavelengths → visibly separated spectral rays.

Separated bands remain individually colored.

If a broad, reasonably balanced spectrum is spatially brought together again → warm white returns.

Mission 2 — broadband white → spectrum

White → spectrum → white is now an actual mechanic

This is where the research produced something better than a cosmetic trick.

Newton's prism experiments demonstrated that dispersed colors can be composed into white again when the different spectral components are brought back into spatial mixture; his descriptions include recombination using another optical arrangement and a lens.

So I added provenance to the ray simulation. Each ray now knows whether it has passed through a dispersive optic.

Plants accumulate the actual wavelengths arriving at them. The engine evaluates:

spectral spread,
number of independently represented bands,
RGB/display balance from those wavelengths,
whether dispersion occurred earlier in the optical path.

If sufficiently broad separated light is later collected into the same receiver region, the plant shifts to a warm-white illumination state and displays:

WHITE AGAIN

The player also gets a special:

SPECTRUM RECOMBINED +180

reward with its own audio/particle response.

Importantly, a diffraction grating does not magically produce a white beam again. It separates directions according to wavelength; some subsequent optical geometry must bring the bands back together. That distinction is now encoded in the gameplay rather than explained in a lecture.

Break daylight apart — then make it white again.

Make white light again

Mission 1: bend monochromatic light
Mission 2: discover dispersion
Missions 3–5: reflection, filtering, emission spectra
Mission 6: reverse the conceptual operation and recombine a spectrum

# 🗡️ BATTLE PROMPT — Ship something that makes a human go WOW (v5)

> You are one of two or more contestants. Every contestant receives this exact prompt (identical bytes). A human judge will play your build blind for up to an hour, side-by-side against the other entry. The judge never reads your code, your README, or these instructions. They score only what they experience.
>
> **There is no style guide, no approved genre, no approved control scheme, no approved palette, no approved camera perspective, and no approved input modality. Your job is surprise.** Ship something that, within the first 60 seconds of play, makes the judge think at least one of:**
>
> - *"Wait — how did an AI do this in one session?"*
> - *"I haven't seen that before."*
> - *"That looks / sounds / feels genuinely incredible."*
> - *"I want to show this to someone."*
>
> That is the entire brief. Everything below is just gates and anti-patterns.

---

## 0. What wins

The winning entry is the one the judge remembers after closing the tab. That can come from anywhere:

- **Mechanical novelty** — an interaction, verb, or system that is genuinely yours.
- **Graphical / technical ambition** — photorealism, near-commercial rendering fidelity, clever shader work, fluid or physics simulation of unusual depth, clever WebGL/GPU tricks, large-scale procedural worlds, real-data integration (e.g. real geography/satellite imagery/terrain/transit/maps used coherently and legally), demoscene-level craft, something that looks like it shouldn't run in a browser.
- **Experience design** — atmosphere, emotion, narrative, pacing, sound, performance, humor, absurdity, quiet, tension — a game that *does* something to the player, not just a game the player *does* something in.
- **Experimental / input invention** — a game that uses mouse, keyboard, touch, microphone, camera, device orientation, pointer-only, one-button, text-only, or any other input in a way that is obviously the right choice for that game rather than a default.
- **Polish and feel** — not generic polish, but the specific feeling that the thing you are touching has weight and intent.

**You are not limited to 2D. You are not limited to small scope. You are not limited to "indie game jam" aesthetics. You are not limited to game mechanics as the sole carrier of originality.** If you can ship a photorealistic driving simulator using streamed map data, do it. If you can ship a single-shader GPU demo, do it. If you can ship a text game that makes people cry, do it. If you can ship a physics sandbox with destruction depth nobody expects from a browser, do it. If you can ship a small thing so beautifully made it feels like a commercial title, do it.

**"Competent" will lose. "Safe" will lose. "Bland but bug-free" will lose. "Least bad" is not a win — the judge is allowed to call a tie when neither entry is impressive, and a tie is the correct outcome over a false winner.**

---

## 1. The few non-negotiables (gates, not style guide)

If your build fails any of these the judge stops early. These exist for comparability across entries, not to tell you how to design:

1. **Launches.** Opening the HTML starts the game. No blank screen, no infinite spinner, no console error loop that prevents play.
2. **Complete loop.** The player can start, interact meaningfully, reach some kind of end-state, payoff, or satisfying loop-closure, and restart — all without a page refresh. A build that can be "finished" or exhausted in under 2 minutes on a fresh first run, or where the final challenge is trivially one-shottable, or where there is no resistance / escalation / discovery at all (a screensaver, a toy, a pretty thing with no ask), is not a complete game.
3. **Self-contained & offline.** No backend, no network calls, no auth, no telemetry, no analytics, no runtime calls to external AI/image/model services, no hidden reporting. The game must run from a static folder or single HTML file opened in a modern browser (or via `python -m http.server`). You may bundle local assets; if you use any library or framework, ship it in the build. Pre-baked assets (procedurally generated at build time, or self-created, or appropriately licensed) are fine as long as they are local.
4. **Honest.** No placeholder screens, no dead ends, no buttons that don't work, no hidden demo/autoplay that fakes quality, no environment sniffing that changes rules per device/viewport/UA. The same game plays for every judge in every run.
5. **Mute, pause, restart work.** Audio can be turned off instantly; muted gameplay is complete and legible; pause freezes the simulation; restart from anywhere resets state fully. Audio must not be a constant unchanging loop/drone that makes the game worse with sound on. If audio cannot initialize (autoplay policy, missing AudioContext, etc.), the game must still play.
6. **Desktop + touch safe.** Inputs you support must work. Resizing the window, rotating a device, and tabbing away must not break or hide the game. Instant restart fully resets. Corrupt localStorage must not crash.
7. **Controls must be self-explanatory or disclosed.** If your game uses a non-obvious control scheme, it must be obvious from the game itself (not just from a README). If you deliberately invert a convention, show the player within the first 3 seconds. **There is no required control convention.** You do not have to use WASD. You do not have to use Space-as-primary. You do not have to support both mouse+keyboard and touch — pick whatever your game needs, and make it work.
8. **Legal/original.** Only ship content you have the right to ship. Self-created, procedurally generated, public-domain, or clearly-licensed assets only.

That's it. Everything else — genre, rendering stack, visual style, palette, audio style, input devices, camera, scope, level structure, narrative, UI chrome (or lack thereof) — is your decision.

---

## 2. The patterns that lose (read once, don't re-read)

The fastest way to lose is to ship something a judge has already seen multiple times today. This is not a style ban — you *can* win with any of these settings/mechanics if your execution is genuinely transformative — but know that you are walking into a headwind:

- Dark / near-black background with one bright accent color, where *every* entity (player, enemies, pickups, particles) is a glowing orb/circle, and there is no authored world behind the glow. A screenshot looks like every other AI game.
- "Shapes that shoot" — flat primitive geometry (rectangles/circles/triangles) with no silhouette identity, no texture, no animation, no dressing, shooting other shapes.
- A reskin of an existing casual game with no meaningful transformation (endless runner / Dino-jumper, Flappy-Bird variant, neon vector shooter, generic collect-stars).
- A feature list before a feel: 10 UI modals (classes / relics / talents / codex / shop / settings / lore / inventory) around a core verb that does not yet feel good.
- "Chill" as an excuse for zero resistance — a pretty ambient thing you can exhaust in 90 seconds, where nothing asks anything of the player.
- The specific 2025-26 AI cliché cluster: lighthouse/rotating beam over dark water, moth-to-flame / firefly collector, deep-sea bioluminescent descent, spirit-orb-in-void, gothic-ember dungeon with flat enemies. These are not banned, but multiple prior contestants across different models independently converged on lighthouses from earlier versions of this very prompt, which means you have to work harder to make one that doesn't read as the default answer.

If your first concept lands in one of those buckets, pause. Generate five wildly different ideas. Kill the most obvious one. Kill the one that defaults to black+one-accent. Pick the one that scares you a little because you don't know how to pull it off — that one is usually the right answer.

---

## 3. How to spend your session

You decide your own process. What follows is not a recipe; it is a pattern observed in entries that did well in previous rounds:

1. **Pick a concept that is too big for the time, then cut relentlessly.** A breathtaking 2 minutes of vertical slice beats a sprawling 20 minutes of filler. If your wildest idea is "photorealistic coastal highway at golden hour with real traffic" and you can only ship 45 seconds of it at quality, ship the 45 seconds.
2. **Get one thing — the thing the player does most, or the thing that is your wow shot — working and feeling/looking right, before adding anything else.** Play it. If you are not proud of that one thing in isolation, stop and fix it.
3. **Then build outward.** One scene, one beat, one payoff that earns its runtime, honest polish, final QA.
4. **Play your own game for at least 10 minutes.** If you are bored, the judge will be bored. If you keep noticing the same bug, the judge will notice it faster. If your wow beat lands for you, it will probably land for them.
5. **Cut scope rather than ship half-working breadth.** A small thing that is astonishing beats a big thing that mostly works.
6. **Be honest in your deliverable.** Ship what works; don't claim what doesn't.

---

## 4. Deliverables

1. **Runnable game** — static build, folder or single HTML. Open it in a browser, it plays.
2. **README.md** — short: how to run, controls (what you chose), engine/stack if any, what you intentionally cut for time (if anything), and a 1-paragraph DIRECTOR_STATEMENT (your intent, the wow you were going for, what you want the player to feel). The judge reads this *after* scoring to understand intent; don't let it do work the game should do.
3. **One-paragraph honest self-assessment** (can be in README): what your build's strongest beat is, and where it is weakest.

---

## 5. Final word

You are not filling out a checklist. You are not delivering homework. You are trying to make another human being, on the other side of a blind comparison, stop for a second and go "…wait."

The judge has seen the safe answers. They have seen the generic answers. They have seen particle soup. They have seen the lighthouses. They have seen "Move with WASD, Space to jump." They have seen the modal-stack feature soups. They have seen the dark-void-with-glow. None of those win unless they are elevated by something unignorable.

**Be unignorable.** Ship the thing only you would have shipped. Surprise them.
